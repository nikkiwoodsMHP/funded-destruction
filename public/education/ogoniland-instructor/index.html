Module 01 · Instructor guide

Who Pays for Environmental Destruction?

A 50–60-minute source-evaluation lesson using UNEP's 2011 Ogoniland environmental assessment.

Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson students should be able to:

  1. Describe at least two findings documented by UNEP in 2011.
  2. Distinguish environmental harm, operational responsibility, financial connection, and legal liability.
  3. Evaluate an environmental claim using dated, attributable sources.
  4. Formulate a specific, researchable financial-accountability question.

Materials and preparation

  • Student brief (printed or digital)
  • UNEP 2011 executive summary, ideally pre-read
  • Access to a browser or printed source extracts
  • Optional short video chosen and verified by the instructor; do not treat videos as proof without checking attribution
Instructor note: Some students may have direct experience of pollution, conflict, or displacement. Offer an alternative case analysis or an option not to disclose personal experiences.

Suggested lesson plan

0–7 minutes

Opening question

“Who pays when environmental damage occurs?” Collect hypotheses without treating them as conclusions.

7–17 minutes

Review the evidence

Students locate two findings in UNEP's summary and record what the source actually says.

17–32 minutes

Build an accountability map

In groups, distinguish operators, financiers, regulators, and cleanup funders. Mark unverified links with a question mark.

32–47 minutes

Financial research sprint

Students propose specific records needed for tracing financial relationships, including the entity name, period, and record type.

47–55 minutes

Debrief and ethics

Discuss what evidence is missing, and why media headlines may oversimplify complex responsibility.

55–60 minutes

Exit ticket

One verified fact, one unknown, one next research source.

Facilitator guidance

What strong answers should show

  • Evidence: Students correctly attribute contamination findings to UNEP's 2011 assessment rather than presenting them as today's measurements.
  • Financial literacy: Students understand that owning equity does not by itself show that an investor financed a particular incident, and that cleanup pledges differ from paid amounts.
  • Critical thinking: Students distinguish legal findings from ethical arguments and possible investigative leads.
  • Source quality: Students identify author, date, publication, and the passage supporting a claim.

Discussion prompts to extend learning

  1. If a company reports a cleanup pledge, what evidence would prove that the money was actually disbursed?
  2. What sources might allow communities to challenge official or corporate accounts?
  3. Why might loans, equity ownership, operating contracts, and insurance have different accountability implications?
  4. Should the same standard of evidence apply to headlines, classroom essays, and public allegations? Why?

Quick rubric: 0–2 points per category

Accuracy

2 = claims precise, sourced, and dated; 1 = partially supported; 0 = unsupported.

Financial reasoning

2 = roles clearly distinguished; 1 = some conflation; 0 = unsupported attribution.

Evidence evaluation

2 = source limits identified; 1 = partial; 0 = none.

Research question

2 = specific and answerable; 1 = vague; 0 = absent.

Maximum 8 points. Formative classroom assessment, not a validated standardized instrument.

Source notes and editorial boundaries

This lesson uses UNEP's published 2011 investigation as its factual base. The suggested $1 billion initial cleanup fund was a recommendation, not evidence that the full amount was paid. UNEP's findings of contamination do not alone constitute a legal determination of ecocide by any particular actor.
